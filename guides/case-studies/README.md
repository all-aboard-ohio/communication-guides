# How to Create Compelling Case Studies

**Subtopic:** Local stories with measurable impact

## Overview

Case studies are one of the most powerful tools in an advocate's communication toolkit. They ground abstract data in the real experiences of real communities, making the benefits of rail and transit investments concrete and relatable. This guide will help you document local wins — from ridership increases to community revitalization — in a way that inspires future advocacy campaigns.

## Goals

- Help advocates ground abstract data in the real experiences of real communities
- Make benefits concrete by connecting rail outcomes to people, places, and measurable change
- Build a growing library of documented local wins that inspire future advocacy campaigns

## Key Talking Points

### 1. Story Structure

Every compelling case study follows a clear narrative arc:

- **Open with a person or place** — Start with a specific individual, neighborhood, or business that has been positively affected. This hooks the reader emotionally.
- **Build with data** — Introduce quantitative outcomes (e.g., ridership growth, travel time savings, economic impact) to support the human story.
- **Close with a call to action** — End by inviting the reader to take the next step: support a campaign, contact a legislator, or share the story.

### 2. Data Integration

Weaving quantitative outcomes into a narrative without losing the human thread:

- Use data to *amplify* the story, not replace it. For example: "Before the BRT line, Maria spent 90 minutes commuting each way. Now it takes 25 minutes."
- Visualize key metrics (e.g., before/after charts) when possible.
- Always cite sources for your data to maintain credibility.

### 3. Stakeholder Voices

Gathering and incorporating quotes, testimony, and endorsements:

- Identify diverse stakeholders: riders, business owners, local officials, community leaders.
- Use the interview guide (see Resources) to capture authentic, quotable material.
- Attribute quotes clearly and obtain permission before publishing.

### 4. Measured Outcomes

Defining and tracking what success looks like before and after:

- Establish baseline metrics before the intervention (e.g., ridership, commute times, property values).
- Document the same metrics after the intervention.
- Highlight both quantitative (e.g., 40% increase in ridership) and qualitative (e.g., "I can get to work on time now") outcomes.
