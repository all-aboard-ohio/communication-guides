# Translating Technical Reports for General Audiences

**Subtopic:** Making dense research digestible

## Overview

This guide helps advocates turn lengthy, jargon-heavy technical reports—such as environmental impact statements, feasibility studies, and planning documents—into compelling, shareable narratives. By following these practices, you can lower the barrier for non-expert community members to engage with and communicate technical findings, while maintaining accuracy and maximizing reach.

## Goals

- Help advocates distill 100-page reports into clear, actionable takeaways
- Enable consistent formatting of summaries that are accessible to general audiences
- Preserve the integrity of the original data while making it understandable

## Key Techniques

### 1. Key Finding Extraction

Identify the three to five most impactful takeaways from any report:

- **Read the executive summary first** — it often contains the core conclusions.
- **Look for quantitative claims** — numbers that affect people’s daily lives (e.g., commute times, cost estimates, safety statistics).
- **Ask “So what?”** — for each finding, articulate why it matters to a resident, business owner, or local official.
- **Prioritize findings that drive action** — focus on what can be influenced or changed.

### 2. Jargon Elimination

Substitute technical language with plain English equivalents:

| Technical Term | Plain English |
|----------------|---------------|
| Level of Service (LOS) | Traffic flow rating |
| Vehicle Miles Traveled (VMT) | How much people drive |
| Environmental Impact Statement (EIS) | Environmental review report |
| Right-of-way | Public land along roads |
| Mode share | How people travel (car, bus, bike, walk) |
| Induced demand | More roads can lead to more traffic |

**Rule of thumb:** If your grandmother wouldn’t understand it, rephrase it.

### 3. Visual Summarization

Replace dense data tables with charts and infographics:

- **Bar charts** — compare categories (e.g., ridership by route).
- **Line graphs** — show trends over time (e.g., traffic growth).
- **Pie charts or stacked bars** — show proportions (e.g., mode share).
- **Infographics** — combine icons, short text, and simple visuals to tell a story.

**Tip:** Use free tools like Canva, Datawrapper, or Google Charts to create visuals without design experience.

### 4. Writing Compelling Headlines and Summaries

Capture attention without distorting findings:

- **Headline:** State the most surprising or impactful result. Example: “New Study Shows Bus Rapid Transit Could Cut Commute Times by 20%”
- **Summary (first paragraph):** Answer who, what, where, when, and why in two to three sentences.
- **Body:** Present key findings in order of importance. Use short paragraphs and bullet points.
- **Call to action:** Tell readers what they can do next (e.g., attend a meeting, share the summary).

## Example Use Case

A volunteer advocate receives a 120-page NEPA environmental assessment and needs to present its key findings at a community meeting in two days. Using this guide:

1. **Extract** the three most impactful findings (e.g., projected traffic increase, noise impacts, proposed mitigation).
2. **Eliminate jargon** — replace “Level of Service” with “traffic flow” and “mitigation measures” with “solutions to reduce noise.”
3. **Create visuals** — a bar chart comparing current vs. projected traffic, and a simple map showing affected areas.
4. **Write a one-page summary** with a compelling headline, key findings, and a call to action for community input.

Result: A clean, accurate summary and three supporting visuals that empower the community to engage meaningfully.
